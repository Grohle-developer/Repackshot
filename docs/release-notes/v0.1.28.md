# Repackshot 0.1.28

Repackshot is a free, source-available 3D product renderer, in the style of KeyShot. It runs as a desktop app and in the browser, and it renders with its own engine, written in Rust with no dependencies.

**This is a preview release.** Its new features:

- **liquids**: a part becomes water, oil, honey, milk and more, flows into the glass that holds it, and renders with the right refraction;
- **water that reads as water**: drops leave a continuous wet streak instead of a line of beads, and are drawn at their real size;
- **the cloth, shown as measured**: new pictures of how the fabrics drape on the textile laboratory's tester, and the cap of a bottle under silk.

## What's new

### Liquids

- **Convert to liquid.** Right-click a part ▸ *Convert to liquid* ▸ *Water*, *Oil*, *Honey*, *Milk*, *Gel*, *Syrup*, *Orange juice* or *Red wine*. The part is hidden and a liquid part takes its shape, with the preset's look. *Start* in the physics bar fills it with particles and pours it into the parts that hold it and onto the floor: each liquid flows as it should, honey slow and clinging, water quick to level.
- **A litre stays a litre.** Poured into a closed glass, the volume of the rendered surface stays within about 1.5 % of what was poured, and honey takes more than five times as long as water to level. Nothing goes through the glass.
- **It is recorded** on the timeline like a fall, and each frame's surface shows in *Fast*, in *Full* and in the render. One undo step converts, another the run; *Reset* takes it away.
- **A *Liquid* section** in the inspector changes the liquid and the particle size (automatic from the part's volume, or your own), and says how many particles to expect and, after a run, how many there are.
- **A liquid inside its glass.** Where a liquid touches the glass that holds it, the render bends the light at the ratio of the two (water in glass), not as two surfaces against air, so there is no bright line of air at the contact. A glass material has a *Nested priority* for overlapping clear solids (0, automatic: the glass holds the liquid). With an older render engine, the *Liquid* section says the contact is drawn as against air.

### Water that reads as water

- **A streak, not beads.** A drop running down a part now leaves a continuous wet streak behind it, not a dotted line of equal beads. Only a drop running fast over a surface that water wets well (paper, for example) sheds a few small pearls, far apart and of different sizes.
- **Drops drawn their real size.** The outline of every drop is its contact with the surface in every state, so two drops that just merged, or a drop hanging from an edge, no longer look larger (or smaller) than they are. A hanging drop stays where its contact line is and stretches below it.
- **The right surface for each part.** The example's metallic paint now wets as paint, not as metal, and its paper label as paper; clear-coated paints wet as paint and the catalogue's paper and board as paper.

### The cloth, as measured

- **The cap stays under the cloth.** The cloth collides with the sharp edges of what it covers, so the rim of a bottle's cap no longer shows through silk between the cloth's points.
- **Fabrics drape as fabrics.** On Cusick's drape tester (a 300 mm disc held on a 180 mm one), silk hangs in thirteen narrow waves and the heavier fabrics in fewer and broader folds, with the drape coefficients that textile laboratories measure for each kind.

### Updates

- **The update window's text follows the interface's text size.** The window that asks about a newer version at startup used fixed pixel sizes, so it ignored the *Text size* setting; it now follows it like the rest of the interface.

## Downloads

- **Repackshot-0.1.28-windows-x64-portable.zip**: Windows portable. Unzip it and run `repackshot.exe`. Data stays in the `data` folder next to the program.
- **Repackshot-0.1.28-windows-x64-setup.exe**: Windows installer, for your user only and without administrator. Data goes to `%LOCALAPPDATA%\Repackshot`.
- **Repackshot-0.1.28-linux-x64-portable.tar.gz**: Linux portable; it needs Chromium, Chrome, Edge or Brave (and glibc 2.38 or later for the AI).
- **SHA256SUMS.txt**: the SHA-256 sums of the three packages. On Linux: `sha256sum -c SHA256SUMS.txt`; in PowerShell: `Get-FileHash .\Repackshot-0.1.28-windows-x64-setup.exe -Algorithm SHA256`.

The film simulation plugin does not change: it is still `film-0.1.0.rpk`, the asset of the 0.1.6 release.

The `.exe` is not signed: SmartScreen may stop the first start (**More info**, then **Run anyway**).

## Known limitations

- **Liquids.**
  - The containers are the parts that stay still: a moving body is not a container, the liquid does not push it, and a cloth is not a container either.
  - Two liquids in one run do not meet: each flows as if it were alone.
  - Rain, dust and snow skip a liquid, and rain falls through it. The *Weather* switches show on a liquid part but change nothing.
  - It is slow in the browser: about 4,000 particles take roughly seven seconds of computing per simulated second, and a run stops by itself at 20 simulated seconds.
  - The surface is a smoothed density: thin sheets and drops smaller than about two particles are lost. There is no foam or bubbles. *Fast* draws the liquid with the preview's glass, without the nested indices, so honey reads greyer there than in the render.
- **Water.**
  - A drop on a level face never slides, so it keeps growing; real water would hold at the rim and then spill over, and big puddles are still drawn as caps.
  - The wettability values are those of clean laboratory surfaces, not weathered ones.
- **Cloth.**
  - A body falling on a cloth goes through it: the coupling is one-way.
  - It takes longer than it simulates: the sheet *Drape over the part* lays over the example takes about seven to eleven seconds of computing per simulated second in the browser's engine.
  - A stiff, heavy fabric on a very fine sheet bends somewhat more than its rigidity says, and some drapes keep creeping slowly; the run then stops at its time limit.
- Those of 0.1.27 remain.
